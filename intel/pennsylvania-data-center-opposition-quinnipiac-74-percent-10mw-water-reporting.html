74 Percent of Pennsylvania Voters Oppose a Data Center in Their Town. The State Now Makes Anything Over 10 Megawatts Report Its Water.

A Quinnipiac University poll of 895 Pennsylvania voters found 74 percent opposed to a data center in their community and 16 percent in favor, with 75 percent following AI data center news very or somewhat closely, according to the Pennsylvania Capital-Star. Reporter Ethan Young tracked the organizing behind that number. A Pennsylvania Data Center Resistance group started on Facebook in January passed 500 members in March, added more than a thousand members a week at its peak, and stood at 41,000 on August 7. State Senator Katie Muth's office logged 504 emails about data centers, 182 of them mentioning electricity bills, and 72 of 807 calls on the same topic.

The disclosure threshold landed at 10 megawatts

Lawmakers introduced at least 30 data center bills this session, eight in May and eight more in June. The final budget deal requires any data center above 10 megawatts to file annual energy and water usage reports. Ten megawatts is one AI hall, not one campus, which puts almost every project proposed in the state inside the reporting boundary. Karen Feridun, who helped start the resistance effort, said the response "took off in ways that shocked me." Chris Borick at Lehigh University described it as an issue voters connect directly to their own bills. Pennsylvania spent the past year arguing about water with estimates, because a disclosure bill stalled in the Senate and because farmers near a $586 million nuclear-adjacent project had no filing to point at.

Annual water becomes a number a township can trend

An operator reporting once is a press release. An operator reporting every year builds a series, and a series is what a supervisor holds up at the second hearing. Evaporative cooling consumes water on a curve that tracks summer wet bulb, so year two after a hot August looks worse than year one, and the operator has to explain the shape of the curve rather than the size of a single figure. That is the argument closed-loop and dry-cooled designs win on paper before anyone breaks ground. Hut 8 built Beacon Point closed loop for exactly this reason.

East Whiteland Township in Chester County is the current flashpoint, and it will not be the last. Proposals keep arriving in a state where town halls have already gone badly for the governor's office. The developers who show up in the next twelve months with a published makeup water rate, a tower-free rejection scheme, and a sound study at the property line will move through zoning faster than the ones who show up with an economic impact study. Sixteen percent support is not a number you argue your way out of. It is a number you engineer around.
