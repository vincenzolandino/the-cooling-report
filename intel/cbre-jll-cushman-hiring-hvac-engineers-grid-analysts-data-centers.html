CBRE, JLL and Cushman Are Recruiting HVAC Engineers and Grid Analysts. Site Selection Turned Into a Psychrometric Problem and the Brokerages Noticed.

CBRE, JLL, Cushman & Wakefield and the rest of the global real estate services firms are recruiting fiber optic technicians, electrical and HVAC engineers, and power grid analysts to keep pace with data center development, according to CoStar's reporting. Matt Landek, JLL's global president of data centers and critical environments, described the profile: technical facility knowledge covering power capacity, cooling systems and fiber connectivity, combined with lease negotiation, financial underwriting and tenant relations. Jim Kerrigan, who brokered his first data center lease in 1998 and founded North American Data Centers, now spends his time on thousands of acres of rural land chosen to sit far from any population that would compete with a future campus for power or water.

Kerrigan's shift is a cooling constraint wearing a real estate costume

Converted industrial buildings were a fiber and floor-loading question. Rural acreage at distance from population centers is a water rights and interconnection question, and both of those are set by the thermal design before a broker draws a site plan. Wet bulb sets tower approach and makeup rate. Aquifer depth and municipal allocation set whether that makeup exists. Transmission distance sets whether the campus goes on-grid or builds its own generation, which adds a second cooling load next to the first. Grid constraints are already the binding limit on where these projects land, and the water-for-power trade is the one operators keep getting backwards.

The fee pool is growing faster than the margin

Cushman & Wakefield reported $2.76 billion in second quarter sales against $52.7 million of net income, with growth concentrated in data center and logistics activity across India and the broader Asia Pacific region, per a Simply Wall St analysis published this week. Revenue up, earnings soft. Hiring a power grid analyst costs more than hiring a leasing agent, and the technical bench takes years to build. That gap is the reason specialist advisory shops keep spinning out of the majors.

The practical read for cooling vendors: your buyer is increasingly a brokerage employee with a mechanical background sitting between the operator and the developer, and that person is the one deciding whether your CDU makes a shortlist. Sell to them the way you would sell to a consulting engineer, with approach temperatures, flow rates, and a water balance, rather than the way you would sell to a landlord. The broker who can read a psychrometric chart is going to win the mandate on the next thousand-acre site, and everything downstream of that mandate is a cooling decision.
